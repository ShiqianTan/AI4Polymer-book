Abstract
Polymer informatics currently lacks shared benchmarks
that enable
reproducible comparison of property prediction methods across datasets,
representations, and models. Here, we present PolyBench, a public
benchmark platform comprising 39 literature-derived prediction tasks
with 42,169 curated structure–property records covering 14,717
distinct repeat-unit structures, seven repeat-unit molecular representation
types (five fingerprint families, physicochemical descriptors, and
task-trained graph embeddings), 30 supervised baseline models (22
traditional machine-learning and 8 deep-learning methods), fixed train/test
splits, and an interactive leaderboard. Across more than 10,000 supervised
model evaluations spanning all 39 tasks, physicochemical descriptor-based
tree ensembles emerge as strong baselines under the PolyBench protocol,
though no single method achieves consistent superiority across all
properties; matched repeated-split analyses on three representative
tasks show that the winning configuration is stable across five seeds
for the two larger tasks but not for the smallest, condition-sensitive
task. The current release evaluates repeat-unit-centered polymer property
prediction and does not explicitly encode higher-order factors such
as molecular-weight distributions, tacticity, morphology, processing
history, device architecture, or measurement protocol; tasks are therefore
grouped into three provisional interpretation tiers, with condition-sensitive
tasks explicitly treated as exploratory repeat-unit baselines. PolyBench
is intended as a reproducible infrastructure resource for controlled
method comparison, not as a complete model of chain-, sample-, or
process-dependent polymer behavior, and is available at http://polybench.ciac.jl.cn (http://202.98.16.11)
Keywords: polymer informatics, polymer property prediction, benchmark platform, machine learning, molecular
representation
Introduction
Polymers serve as the ubiquitous material
backbone of modern civilization,
woven inextricably into the fabric of our daily lives and advanced
technology. From the lightweight composites enabling aerospace exploration
to the critical membranes driving green energy storage and the biocompatible
interfaces of medical devices, these materials are indispensable to
societal progress.
−
The functional versatility of polymers arises from their vast chemical
design space; combinatorial variations in monomer chemistry, chain
architecture, sequence composition, and processing conditions create
virtually unlimited structural possibilities.
−
However, this
immense design space simultaneously represents both an opportunity
for innovation and a formidable challenge for materials discovery.
,−
Traditional experimental approaches based on iterative synthesis
and characterization cycles are resource-intensive and time-consuming,
often confining research efforts to incremental optimization within
known chemical families rather than enabling transformative breakthroughs.
,,
Artificial intelligence
offers the potential to fundamentally transform
this paradigm by enabling systematic, data-driven exploration of structure-property
relationships.
,,−
In adjacent fields, particularly inorganic materials
−
and small-molecule drug discovery,
−
standardized benchmarks
have catalyzed rapid methodological advances. These benchmarks provide
curated data sets, canonical tasks, and reproducible evaluation protocols
that transform fragmented individual efforts into coherent, community-wide
progress.
−
While valuable data repositories such as Polymer
Genome and PoLyInfo have aggregated vast amounts of property data, they function
primarily as databases rather than benchmark platforms, lacking the
standardized splits, unified metrics, and canonical task definitions
required for a rigorous algorithmic comparison. A systematic comparison
of PolyBench with Polymer Genome, PoLyInfo, the Citrine platform,
MoleculeNet, Matbench, and representative polymer machine-learning
datasets in terms of task definitions, splits, metadata, licensing,
hidden-test support, source traceability, and reproducibility artifacts
is provided in Supporting Information Table S28. Consequently, despite encouraging advances in predicting thermomechanical
properties, ionic conductivity, and sequence-structure relationships,
−
polymer informatics lacks equivalent benchmarking infrastructure. The absence of standardized data sets, evaluation
protocols, and community-accepted benchmark tasks fundamentally undermines
reproducibility, prevents fair algorithmic comparison, and fragments
research efforts across isolated studies with incompatible methodologies.
Three interconnected challenges necessitate
polymer-specific benchmark
infrastructure. First, data scarcity and fragmentation: polymer datasets
typically contain hundreds to thousands of entries measured under
heterogeneous experimental conditions
,
contrasting
sharply with databases containing >105 molecules in
small-molecule
machine learning. Proprietary industrial
data holdings and inconsistent reporting practices further limit data
integration and accessibility.
−
Second, methodological inconsistency:
disparate train-test splits, evaluation metrics, hyperparameter selection
procedures, and even property definitions render performance claims
across studies incomparable. Canonical benchmark tasks analogous to
those established in MoleculeNet or Matbench do not exist for polymers, preventing systematic
evaluation of methodological advances. Third, representational ambiguity: polymers can be encoded using
multiple representationsSMILES strings (including canonical
SMILES
,
BigSMILES, DeepSMILES, and SELFIES), molecular
fingerprints, graph topologies, or physicochemical descriptor vectors
−
each capturing different structural and chemical features.
Without controlled comparisons under standardized conditions, optimal
representation-algorithm pairings remain unclear and context-dependent.
Polymers present distinct scientific challenges
compared with materials
covered by established benchmarks. Unlike discrete small molecules
or crystalline inorganic materials with deterministic structures,
polymers exist as complex ensembles characterized by distributions
of molecular weight, chain architecture, tacticity, and supramolecular
morphology. Property measurements depend critically on processing
history, thermal treatment, and measurement protocols, introducing
reproducibility challenges absent in simpler material classes.
,
Consequently, polymer benchmarks require purpose-built infrastructure
that explicitly addresses this complexity while maintaining rigorous
standardization and reproducibility.
Here we introduce PolyBench (Figure
), a reproducible benchmark platform for repeat-unit-based
polymer property prediction built around four components: (1) a curated
dataset infrastructure of 39 literature-derived tasks with 42,169
property records;
,
(2) standardized polymer representations
spanning fingerprints, physicochemical descriptors, and graph-based
embeddings; (3) documented predictive baselines together with auxiliary
diagnostic analyses; and (4) a unified evaluation infrastructure with
fixed splits, common metrics, and interactive leaderboards. The present
benchmark is designed for a controlled comparison of property prediction
workflows under matched data artifacts and evaluation protocols. Within
this scope, PolyBench provides reproducible baselines and reveals
benchmark-dependent trends in task difficulty, representation choice,
and model performance while also highlighting important limitations
associated with higher-order polymer structure, data provenance, and
experimental-condition heterogeneity.

Results and Discussion
We structured our investigation
into four logical phases: We begin
by characterizing the curated experimental dataset that serves as
the foundation of PolyBench, followed by detailing the automated benchmarking
framework designed to ensure reproducibility. Subsequently, we present
a systematic analysis of predictive performance across properties,
representations, and algorithms, characterizing benchmark-dependent
performance patterns, and identifying strong baseline approaches within
this setting. Finally, we introduce the public Web platform that makes
these resources available to the community.
Curated Repeat-Unit-Level Dataset Spanning Diverse Polymer Properties
The reliability of any data-driven benchmark is fundamentally bounded
by the quality, provenance, and representativeness of its underlying
data. PolyBench assembles curated polymer structure-property records
from peer-reviewed literature, public polymer data resources, and
validated laboratory measurements where available, including previously
compiled polymer-property datasets used in machine-learning studies.
,
We use the term “property record” rather than a universal
“experimental measurement” because the source character
can differ across tasks, especially for electronic properties whose
values may originate from experimental reports, computational studies,
or database-derived estimates depending on the original source. The
dataset encompasses 39 distinct prediction tasks organized into 6
property categories: thermal, physicochemical, electrical, mechanical,
optical, and solution properties (Figures
, ; complete property
definitions, abbreviations, and units in Supplementary Table S23). Representative tasks include glass transition temperature
(T
g
) and melting temperature
(T
m
) for thermal behavior;
Young’s modulus and tensile strength for mechanical performance;
and bandgap and ionization energy for electrical characteristics.

To quantify the landscape of available experimental
data and identify
coverage gaps, we analyzed the distribution of sample sizes and property
categories across the platform (Figure
). Data set sizes span three orders of magnitude (193
to 8,055 samples), directly reflecting differences in experimental
accessibility, measurement standardization, and historical reporting
frequency across property classes. T
g,
the most extensively studied polymer property, comprises 8,055 measurements,
while specialized properties such as loss modulus and specific heat
capacity contain 193 and 207 entries, respectively. Thermal properties,
electrical properties, and physicochemical properties each account
for the highest share at 25.6%, reflecting their central importance
in polymer characterization and industrial applications. This distribution
enables systematic investigation of machine-learning performance across
the small-to-moderate data regime characteristic of experimental polymer
science, where obtaining sufficient high-quality measurements remains
a fundamental bottleneck.
To characterize chemical diversity
beyond per-task sample counts,
we canonicalized every repeat-unit SMILES across the platform. The
39 task files contain 42,169 structure–property rows covering
14,717 globally distinct canonical repeat-unit structures; task-wise
unique-structure counts, Murcko-scaffold counts, and empty-scaffold
fractions are listed in Supplementary Tables S30 and S33. Because a polymer can appear in more than one task,
703 of the 741 task pairs share at least one structure, and four pairs
(dielectric constant/refractive index, density/specific volume, electron
affinity/ionization energy, and solubility parameter/cohesive energy
density) have canonical-SMILES Jaccard coefficients above 0.94, reflecting
shared task membership rather than an algebraic relation between the
paired targets (Supplementary Table S34). Within-task pairwise Tanimoto similarity distributions on Morgan
fingerprints are reported in Supplementary Table S37; across 32 of the 39 tasks, the median pairwise similarity
is 0.10–0.15, indicating broad internal diversity, whereas
PCE (median 0.28) and T
Lc
(median 0.22) are the most internally homogeneous tasks.
We
implemented a three-stage standardization protocol to improve
data consistency and reproducibility. Data cleansing: exact duplicate
entries and obvious transcription errors were identified and removed
where they could be resolved from the available record. When repeated
values for the same repeat unit and target property were not accompanied
by sufficient metadata to distinguish molecular weight, sample history,
or measurement protocol, we treated them conservatively as part of
the residual label heterogeneity rather than as evidence for a single
definitive value. Unit and label normalization: Properties were converted
to canonical units (e.g., T
g
in ◦C, moduli in GPa, energies in eV) using
explicit conversion rules, and property labels were mapped to a unified
ontology while preserving original annotations where available. Chemical
validation: SMILES strings representing polymer repeat units were
parsed, validated, and canonicalized using RDKit v2023.09.1; entries producing parsing failures or chemically
unreasonable structures were flagged for manual inspection and excluded
if unresolvable. This workflow reduces preprocessing variability that
can confound algorithmic comparisons in the literature. A per-task
curation audit comparing the raw ingest files with the deposited task
files is provided in Supplementary Table S32: across all 39 files, 42,169 rows canonicalize successfully with
no RDKit parse failures, and four exact-duplicate rows (all in the
PCE task) are retained because the stored benchmark results were generated
from these files. A structure-level replicate audit (Supplementary Table S38) shows that only the PCE task contains
canonical-SMILES duplicate groups (59 groups involving 12.2% of its
rows, with a median within-group standard deviation of 0.61 percentage
points of PCE); the remaining 38 task files contain none. Separate
per-record counts of transcription and unit-conversion corrections
were not retained during the original curation and therefore cannot
be reported retrospectively. However, literature-derived polymer data
inevitably retain irreducible heterogeneity arising from molecular-weight
distributions, tacticity, sample processing, and measurement conditions
(e.g., scan rate for thermal transitions, frequency and temperature
for dielectric properties, gas identity and pressure for transport
properties). Moreover, the current task files do not retain row-level
source-paper identifiers or experimental-versus-computational labels;
per-task source counts, experimental-versus-computational fractions,
source-resolved replicate dispersion, and source-bias controls therefore
cannot be reconstructed from the present files and remain a required
step for future releases. Cross-task performance differences should
accordingly be read as reflecting label provenance, sample size, and
source overlap as well as target physics. For the current release,
PolyBench retains records compatible with the stated task definition
when no explicit nonstandard protocol is indicated and interprets
condition-sensitive tasks more cautiously through provisional interpretation
tiers. Some condition-induced variance nevertheless remains and motivates
future condition-aware benchmark design.
To guide interpretation,
we group the 39 tasks into three provisional
interpretation tiers (Supplementary Table S27): Tier 1 (14 tasks), Tier 2 (15 tasks), and Tier 3 (10 tasks). Assignment
is based on four considerationssample coverage, expected sensitivity
to unrecorded conditions, approximate measurement standardization,
and source heterogeneityrather than on property category alone;
observed model performance is deliberately excluded as a criterion
so that a tier is not defined and validated by the same scores. Tier
1 collects tasks for which repeat-unit composition is expected to
provide a comparatively informative baseline, including T
g
, T
d
, and T
m
, band-gap
and frontier-energy targets, and dielectric constant. Tier 2 is intermediate
and includes selected thermal and physicochemical properties, gas-transport
coefficients, tensile properties, and PCE. Tier 3 is explicitly the
exploratory, condition-sensitive group, covering properties expected
to depend more strongly on measurement frequency, temperature, or
sample history, such as IWLT, conductivity, dielectric loss, and dynamic
mechanical properties; results for these tasks should be read as repeat-unit
baselines rather than predictions for fully specified polymer samples.
No tier is claimed to be proven repeat-unit-dominated; the tiers are
expert-judgment aids that we expect to refine as condition-aware metadata
become available.
All 39 tasks follow standardized protocols
to improve reproducibility
and fair comparison within the repeat-unit-level setting: canonical
SMILES input representation, fixed 70/30 train/test split with version-controlled
indices, grouped cross-validation for hyperparameter optimization
where supported, and unified evaluation metrics (R
2, MAE, RMSE). The same train/test indices are used across
representations and models for a given task. Grouping is based on
Murcko scaffold labels where meaningful, which reduces some near-neighbor
leakage but does not fully eliminate all polymer-specific leakage
modes, such as homologous-series effects, common source-paper effects,
or repeat-unit variants with similar chemistry. We therefore interpret
the current split as a fixed community baseline rather than as a complete
out-of-domain generalization test. To probe robustness beyond this
fixed split, the revision adds four complementary diagnostics on representative
high-, medium-, and low-data tasks (T
g
, 8,055 rows; chain bandgap
Egc
, 3,380 rows; loss modulus E″, 193 rows): (i) matched five-seed repeated 70/30 splits
with fixed model configurations; (ii) a Murcko-group-disjoint auxiliary
split; (iii) a strict similarity-threshold split that caps the maximum
train–test Tanimoto similarity at 0.6; and (iv) a chemical-family
holdout that assigns whole PoLyInfo-style families to train or test, together with dummy, median, and nearest-neighbor
baselines and percentile-bootstrap confidence intervals (Supplementary Tables S27, S29–S30, S36, S39, S42–S46; Methods). Source-grouped splits could not be
constructed because the current task files lack row-level source-paper
identifiers; they are scheduled for a future release. Critically,
the released tabular representationsfingerprints and physicochemical
descriptorsare precomputed, version-controlled, and distributed
alongside exact train/test indices, while task-trained graph embeddings
are generated within the corresponding training pipelines and documented
separately (see Methods; detailed property
definitions and units in Supplementary Table S23). This mirrors the community-level specification philosophy established
by Matbench and substantially reduces
preprocessing as a confounding variable.
Automated Benchmarking Framework Integrating Diverse Algorithms
and Representations
Having established the data foundation,
we next constructed a standardized computational workflow to ensure
a rigorous algorithmic comparison. PolyBench provides an end-to-end
automated workflow spanning task selection, molecular encoding, model
training, hyperparameter optimization, and a standardized evaluation.
This design systematically eliminates ad hoc implementation differences
that confound literature comparisons by ensuring that all methods
are evaluated under identical conditions with version-controlled data,
representations, and random seeds.
The platform compares seven
input representation types capturing complementary aspects of polymer
structure and chemistry: (1) five fingerprint families (AtomPair,
Morgan, RDKit, Torsion, MACCS keys) encoding substructural motifs
and topological patterns as fixed-length binary or count vectors;
(2) one physicochemical descriptor family (Mordred suite, >1,800
descriptors)
capturing molecular properties including topological indices, electronic
structure parameters, and thermodynamic estimates (Supplementary Tables S1–S9); and (3) one task-trained
graph-embedding family learning topology-aware representations through
message passing on molecular graphs. Among the released precomputed
tabular artifacts, six representation families are provided, excluding
the task-trained graph embeddings, which are generated as part of
the model training pipeline. All precomputed representations are version-controlled
and released with exact generation protocols to guarantee reproducibility
across studies (see Methods and Supplementary Information, Supplementary Figures S15–S28).
The algorithmic ecosystem encompasses 30 supervised predictive
models (22 traditional machine-learning and 8 deep-learning methods;
per-algorithm inventory in Supplementary Table S31) and 12 unsupervised algorithms used for representation
diagnostics and chemical-space exploration. The supervised models
span linear methods (Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet), kernel methods (kernel
ridge regression, support vector regression), gradient-boosting tree
ensembles (Random Forest, ExtraTrees, XGBoost, CatBoost, HistGradientBoosting),
and neural network architectures (multilayer perceptrons, transformers,
recurrent networks, graph neural networks). The unsupervised algorithms
are reported separately because only the supervised models contribute
directly to the predictive benchmark. Hyperparameter grids are fully
documented and fixed across all experiments (Supplementary Tables S12–S22), with optimization performed via exhaustive
or randomized grid search on the training set. Validation details
differ among model families and are documented per script in the released
code; the matched repeated-split analyses described below provide
the controlled comparisons of this work.
We use SHAP analysis
to interpret the model behavior. Evaluation
follows a unified protocol across all tasks: SHAP values are computed
for tree-based models, and attention maps are visualized for transformer
models as diagnostic tools. This framework enables direct comparison
of approximately 280 supervised algorithm-representation evaluations
per task, for a total of more than 10,000 individual model evaluations
across 39 tasks. Together, these results establish baseline performance
and identify the strongest property-specific approaches in the current
benchmark (Supplementary Figures S1–S14).
Systematic Benchmarking Reveals Property-Dependent Predictability
and Identifies Strong Baselines
We first sought to characterize
learnability across the diverse polymer property landscape. Evaluation
across 39 prediction tasks establishes baseline performance references
and identifies property-specific strong approaches within this benchmark.
Performance distributions ranked by held-out test R
2 (Figure
) reveal substantial variation in predictability across property
classes. Electrical properties including chain bandgap
(Egc)
, bulk bandgap
(Egb)
, and electron affinity (E
ea
) exhibit the highest apparent predictability,
with median test R
2 of approximately 0.70–0.80
across algorithm-representation pairs. This pattern may reflect clearer
structure-property relationships for some electronic targets, but
it may also reflect cleaner labels, narrower source heterogeneity,
or computationally derived target values in the underlying literature
sources. We therefore avoid interpreting cross-category differences
as direct measures of intrinsic polymer-property complexity. In contrast,
several mechanical and dynamic-mechanical tasks (e.g., tensile modulus,
storage and loss moduli, loss tangent) and condition-sensitive thermal
and dielectric characteristics (e.g., heat capacity, dielectric loss
factor) show substantially lower medians (median R
2 < 0.3) and greater dispersion, consistent with stronger
sensitivity to molecular weight, processing, morphology, and measurement
protocol; the provisional interpretation tiers broadly track this
ordering (background shading in Figure
). Notably, no single algorithm-representation combination
achieves consistent superiority across all properties, highlighting
the value of systematic benchmarking to identify task-specific baseline
approaches. We caution that for tasks with limited data (N < 500, e.g., loss modulus), performance rankings
should be interpreted as approximate because split stochasticity and
label heterogeneity may influence relative standing more than algorithmic
differences. The repeated-split, baseline, and uncertainty analyses
described below address this concern directly for representative tasks.

and 0.33 eV (0.48 eV) for

were fitted to ln­(E”/GPa) and, after back-transformation,

median-prediction, and one-nearest-neighbor (1-NN, Morgan fingerprints
with Tanimoto similarity) baselines for every task on the fixed split,
recomputed on the matched seeds for the three representative tasks
(Supplementary Tables S27 and S36): XGBoost
reduces mean MAE relative to the matched mean-prediction baseline
by 3.6-fold for T
g
(26.1
vs. 93.6 ◦C) and 4.0-fold for
Egc
(0.33 vs. 1.33 eV), whereas for E″ the margin is only 1.18-fold and the 1-NN baseline
fails to beat the mean predictor on all five seeds, underscoring that
low but positive R
2 values for small,
condition-sensitive tasks should be interpreted cautiously. All six
configurations were fixed before evaluation with no per-split hyperparameter
search, so these conclusions are restricted to the compared configurations.
We further tested whether the random-split rankings persist under
explicit structural separation. In a strict similarity-threshold split
(Supplementary Tables S42–S43),
connected components of a Morgan-fingerprint similarity graph (Tanimoto

cross-set similarity of 0.6; thresholds of 0.4 and 0.5 were also checked,
but giant connected components made an approximately 70/30 component-disjoint
split infeasible for T
g
at those levels. Relative to the matched random splits, XGBoost’s
mean model-scale R
2 decreases from 0.883
to 0.709 for T
g
and from
0.905 to 0.845 for
Egc
, and CatBoost overtakes XGBoost on four
of five T
g
splits. A
complementary chemical-family holdout (Supplementary Tables S44–S46), which assigns whole families from a
deterministic 22-class PoLyInfo-style classifier (89.2% agreement with an independent classification of
a stratified 176-unit sample) to train or test so that no family is
seen during both training and evaluation, shows the same pattern more
strongly: XGBoost’s mean model-scale R

Interactive Web Platform Enables Transparent Evaluation and
Community Contributions
To foster open and collaborative
research, the PolyBench interactive website is now publicly available
at http://polybench.ciac.jl.cn (http://202.98.16.11), as
shown in the Supporting Information. The
root page and static application assets at this URL were verified
as reachable on 25 July 2026; the website is a live HTTP service,
while the Figshare record provides the permanent versioned deposit
(see Data Availability). We distinguish the frozen PolyBench release
from the live web interface: the archived release provides the canonical
data sets, features, split indices, and baseline metadata, whereas
the website provides browsing, visualization, and community-submission
functions. At its core are leaderboards for all 39 tasks, featuring
filtering tools to rank submissions by property category, molecular
representation, algorithm class, data set size, or performance metric.
Each task page presents model cards that document hyperparameters,
training configurations, performance, and available interpretability
analyses. To support reproducibility, the platform provides version-controlled
artifacts for download, including curated data sets, precomputed features,
exact train/test split indices, and model metadata. This enables researchers
to reproduce the official baselines and build upon the same data artifacts.
A guided submission portal supports community participation through
automated validation pipelines that verify schema compliance, split
integrity, and reproducibility requirements (including fixed random
seeds and environment specifications). Official baselines and community
submissions are tracked separately to avoid conflating controlled
reference results with externally optimized methods. Submissions using
external data or additional pretraining must be declared so that leaderboard
comparisons remain interpretable. Because public test labels can encourage
repeated tuning to a fixed benchmark, the current release implements
a hidden-test protocol: the held-out test labels are withheld from
the platform downloads and from the current version of the Figshare
deposit, which provide labeled training partitions together with unlabeled
test structures, and community submissions are scored automatically
on the server against the withheld labels. Because the test labels
of the initial release were openly distributed, the official baseline
results reported here are designated as open-label reference results
and are tracked separately from hidden-test community submissions;
periodically refreshed test sets are planned for future releases as
an additional safeguard against residual exposure to the original
labels. A programmatic API exposes data sets, molecular representations,
data splits, and benchmark results, facilitating meta-analysis, automated
method comparison, and seamless integration into computational workflows.
PolyBench is designed to complement, not duplicate, existing polymer
data resources. PoLyInfo and Polymer
Genome store rich sample- and literature-level
metadata, whereas PolyBench fixes canonical prediction-task definitions,
common splits and metrics, and a shared representation-algorithm catalog
on top of largely preexisting records; canonical repeat-unit SMILES
provide a structural key for cross-checking entries against these
databases (Supplementary Table S28). Long-term
continuity is supported by versioned, citable deposits and documented
reproducibility artifacts: the 39 task CSVs are archived on Figshare
under CC BY 4.0, and the code is released on GitHub under the MIT
License with a pinned environment.yml conda
specification, a HARDWARE.md record of the
server hardware and key package versions, and a step-by-step REPRODUCE.md protocol whose executable scripts regenerate
a complete task result from the deposited T
g
CSVincluding exact split indices, an input
checksum, and platform-compatible prediction filesand whose
validator recomputes all six train/test metrics (test R
2 = 0.851, MAE = 30.4 ◦C for the documented

Methods
Dataset Curation and Standardization
Data Acquisition and Task Definition
PolyBench was
assembled from literature-derived polymer structure–property
records, public polymer data resources
,,
and validated laboratory measurements where available,
including previously compiled datasets used in polymer machine-learning
studies.
,,,,,,
Records were included when the
repeat-unit structure, target property, numerical value, and unit
could be assigned to one of the 39 benchmark tasks. The current 39
task files contain 42,169 rows with a usable repeat-unit string and
target value; a separate file containing 4,978,885 unlabeled polymer
SMILES was used only for representation pretraining and is not included
in any supervised task count. We use the term “property record”
rather than “experimental measurement” because source
character differs across tasks; some electronic targets, for example,
may trace back to experimental reports, computational estimates, or
database-derived values. Entries without a usable repeat-unit representation
or target value, incompatible with the task definition, or chemically
unresolvable after inspection were excluded. Source identifiers, original
labels, and units are retained where available in the released metadata;
row-level source-paper identifiers are not consistently available
across tasks (see Results and Discussion).
Chemical Structure Processing
Polymer structures were
represented by repeat-unit SMILES strings. Structures were parsed, sanitized, and canonicalized with RDKit
v2023.09.1 before the descriptor or fingerprint
calculations. Entries that failed chemical parsing were inspected
and excluded when no chemically reasonable repeat-unit representation
could be assigned. This repeat-unit-level representation provides
a common structural input across tasks, but it does not encode molecular
weight distributions, tacticity, crystallinity, morphology, additives,
device architectures, processing histories, or measurement protocols.
These factors are treated as limitations of the current release rather
than as modeled variables.
Provenance, Unit Normalization, and Quality Control
Property labels and units were mapped to a common ontology, and values
were converted to the canonical units used in the benchmark. Original
annotations were retained, where available. Exact duplicates and clear
transcription errors were removed or corrected when the source record
allowed for resolution. When repeated values for the same repeat unit
and property could not be distinguished by molecular weight, sample
history, or measurement conditions, they were treated as part of the
residual label heterogeneity rather than being collapsed into a single
definitive value. Records with implausible units, inconsistent labels,
or out-of-range values were checked against source documents, where
possible, and excluded only when the issue could not be resolved.
A per-task audit of the deposited files (row counts, columns, target
scale, exact-row duplicates, and RDKit canonicalization outcomes)
is reported in Supporting Information Table S32; it finds four retained exact-duplicate rows in the PCE file, none
in the other 38 task files, and no RDKit parse failures across the
39 task files.
Molecular Representations
PolyBench evaluates seven
repeat-unit representation families. Six are released as precomputed
tabular artifacts: five molecular fingerprint families and one physicochemical
descriptor family. The seventh family consists of task-trained graph
embeddings, which are generated within the corresponding graph model
training pipelines rather than distributed as static feature matrices.
This distinction keeps the released tabular baselines reproducible
while keeping the learned representations tied to their training protocol.
Physicochemical Descriptors and Fingerprints
Physicochemical
descriptors were computed with Mordred v1.2.0 by using two-dimensional repeat-unit structures. The descriptor
matrix was processed using the released workflow: unusable descriptors
were removed, missing values were imputed using training set medians,
and any residual undefined or infinite values were replaced with zero
before model fitting. In the newly added repeated-split analyses,
nonfinite descriptor values were converted to missing values, and
median imputation was fitted on the training rows only. Fingerprint
features were generated with RDKit-based workflows and released alongside the benchmark split indices.
Graph and Sequence Representations
Graph and sequence
models generate learned representations within their supervised training
work flows. Graph models operate on repeat-unit molecular graphs, whereas recurrent and transformer models operate
on SMILES-derived token sequences. Models
that use polymer-SMILES pretraining draw from the separate 4 978 885-row
unlabeled corpus described above. These learned representations are
model-dependent and are therefore reported as part of the supervised
benchmark, rather than as universal precomputed features. Because
pretraining exposure and downstream tuning budgets differ among model
families, comparisons with static descriptor baselines are illustrative
rather than being fully controlled ablations.
Benchmarking Protocol
Data Partitioning and Model Selection
Each task uses
a fixed 70/30 train/test split with version-controlled indices. The
same train/test indices are used across models and molecular representations
for a given task, and training scripts read the pregenerated partition
files directly. Model selection is performed on the training set;
grouped cross-validation with Murcko scaffold labels is used as the group key where supported, but validation
details are model-script-dependent and no single grouped protocol
is shared by every model family (the recorded settings are documented
in the Supporting Information and released
code). This grouping reduces some near-neighbor leakage, but it does
not eliminate polymer-specific leakage modes, such as homologous-series
effects, repeat-unit variants, or shared source papers. The split
should therefore be interpreted as a transparent community baseline
rather than as a strict out-of-domain test.
Repeated-Split, Structural-Split, and Baseline Diagnostics
The repeated-split analysis uses random seeds 0–4 and a
70/30 ratio for T
g
,
Egc
, and E″. For a
given task and seed, CatBoost, XGBoost, ExtraTrees, Random Forest,
SVM, and MLP receive identical row indices. All configurations were
fixed before evaluating the five splits: the four tree methods use
250 boosting iterations or trees; SVM uses an RBF kernel with C = 10 and ϵ = 0.1; MLP has 128-
and 64-unit hidden layers with early stopping. Median imputation is
fitted on training data. SVM and MLP inputs are standardized, and
the MLP additionally uses a training-fitted quantile transform to
limit extrapolation from extreme descriptor values. No per-split hyperparameter
search is performed. Percentile-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals
(10,000 replicates) resample the five seed-level values and therefore
quantify split-to-split variability; paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests
are computed for every model pair within each task and released in
machine-readable form.
As an auxiliary analysis, Murcko scaffolds define groups for one-group-disjoint split per
task. Acyclic repeat units have an empty Murcko scaffold and are assigned
to the same empty-scaffold group. Supporting Information Table S30 reports the number of groups, empty-scaffold fraction,
and realized train/test sizes. This construction tests one form of
structural separation, but repeat-unit scaffolds do not capture molecular
weight, source paper overlap, processing, or morphology; it is therefore
not a strict out-of-domain polymer split.
The strict similarity-threshold
analysis uses Morgan fingerprints
(radius 2, 2,048 bits) to construct an undirected graph for each task.
Two rows are connected when their Tanimoto similarity exceeds 0.6;
connected components are then assigned wholly to train or test in
seed-randomized order until the target 30% test count is reached without
exceeding it. This guarantees a maximum cross-set similarity of 0.6.
Feasibility was checked at threshold values of 0.4, 0.5, and 0.6.
For T
g, the largest components at 0.4
and 0.5 contained 7,727 and 6,460 of 8,055 rows, respectively, precluding
an approximately 70/30 component-disjoint split; 0.6 was the lowest
evaluated threshold feasible for all three tasks.
The chemical-family
holdout assigns each repeat unit one primary
family from the 22-class PoLyInfo scheme using a deterministic RDKit-based classifier: the main chain is
defined as the atoms on a shortest path between the two attachment
points (plus any ring the backbone passes through), functional groups
are matched with SMARTS patterns whose anchor atoms must lie on the
main chain, and a fixed precedence collapses multifamily memberships
to a single primary label. The labels were validated by independently
classifying a stratified 176-unit sample against the same definitions,
with agreement on 157 of 176 units (89.2%) and disagreements confined
to genuinely multifunctional units. A family-disjoint split then assigns
whole families to train or test over five seeds, so no chemical family
is seen during both training and evaluation; no family exceeds 22%
of any of the three tasks, making an approximately 70/30 family-disjoint
split feasible. These structure-based splits do not control the source-paper
identity, processing, molecular weight, or measurement conditions.
The representation ablation trains XGBoost independently on ten
static configurations spanning six families: Mordred descriptors;
Morgan, RDKit, AtomPair, and Torsion fingerprints at 1,024 and 2,048
bits; and MACCS keys. Each configuration uses the same five row partitions,
model target, training-fitted median imputation, and fixed XGBoost
settings as those used for the repeated-split analysis. Paired differences
are computed seed by seed as descriptors minus the comparator; percentile-bootstrap
95% intervals resample these five paired differences and therefore
quantify seed-split uncertainty only. Dummy baselines use the training
set mean or median prediction for every held-out row, and the 1-NN
baseline predicts the target of the most Tanimoto-similar training
structure (Morgan fingerprints, radius 2, 2,048 bits); for the three
repeated-split tasks, all baselines use the same seeds and row indices
as the fitted models.
Algorithms and Hyperparameters
The supervised benchmark
includes 30 predictive models (22 traditional machine-learning and
8 deep-learning methods; per-algorithm inventory in Supporting Information Table S31) across linear, kernel, tree-ensemble,
gradient-boosting
,
neural-network, sequence, and
graph-model families. Twelve unsupervised
algorithms are included separately for representation diagnostics
and chemical space visualization, but they do not contribute directly
to the supervised leaderboard. Model settings and hyperparameter choices
are documented in the Supporting Information and released code. Fixed random seeds are used where supported to
reduce run-to-run variability, although exact numerical reproducibility
can still depend on software versions and hardware.

Conclusions
We have presented PolyBench, a reproducible
benchmark platform
that unifies 39 literature-derived polymer property prediction tasks
under standardized repeat-unit representations, fixed data splits,
and documented baselines. Systematic evaluation reveals that predictive
performance is strongly property-dependent within the PolyBench protocol:
tasks more directly associated with repeat-unit chemistry tend to
be more learnable than tasks dominated by processing, morphology,
device architecture, or measurement conditions. Across the benchmark,
physicochemical descriptor-based tree ensembles provide consistently
competitive official baselines, yet no single method achieves uniform
superiority, underscoring the necessity of task-specific evaluation
over the default model selection. Matched repeated-split analyses
on three representative tasks make one comparison controlled rather
than descriptive: the winning configuration was stable across five
seeds for the two larger tasks, whereas the 193-row loss-modulus task
supported no stable ranking; under strict similarity threshold and
chemical family holdout splits, both absolute performance and model
orderings shifted, quantifying how far random-split results can overstate
out-of-family generalization.
The current release operates on
repeat-unit-level representations
and therefore does not capture molecular-weight distributions, tacticity,
branching, crystallinity, morphology, additives, device architecture,
or processing historyfactors that are critical for many macroscopic
and application-level properties and likely contribute to observed
performance ceilings. The benchmark should therefore not be used to
make definitive claims about morphology- or processing-dominated polymer
behavior without additional metadata. Because the current task files
also lack row-level source identifiers, source-grouped evaluation
remains infeasible in this release, and tuning budgets are not matched
across model families; these constraints bound the strength of any
cross-method conclusion drawn from the stored results. Future releases
will incorporate condition-aware metadata, chain-level descriptors,
row-level source identifiers, and more stringent out-of-domain evaluation
protocols to address these gaps. The accompanying web platform accepts
community submissions through an automated validation pipeline, evaluating
them on the same fixed splits as the official baselines and tracking
them separately so leaderboard comparisons remain interpretable. By
offering version-controlled data artifacts, public baselines, and
an interactive leaderboard, PolyBench provides the polymer informatics
community with a common, auditable reference point for benchmarking
new methods and identifying where current repeat-unit representations
remain insufficient.
